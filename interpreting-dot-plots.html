5.1 Interpreting Dot Plots

A dot plot stacks one dot for each result above a category. The taller the stack, the more there are. But watch the key — sometimes one dot stands for many people. Read the key, spot the highest and lowest stacks, and work out the differences.

Big idea 1

What is a dot plot?

Ages people passed their driving test · 1 dot = 1 person

Data: 17, 17, 20, 17, 22, 18, 22, 18, 22, 18, 22, 17, 19, 18, 18. Each value becomes one dot above that number.

One dot for each result

A dot plot shows data as stacks of dots. Each dot represents a result, and we stack them above the category or number it belongs to.

The frequency of a category is how many dots are in its stack. The tallest stack is the most common; the shortest is the least common.

A gap (a number with no dots) still matters — it tells us nobody had that result.

Big idea 2 · Success criterion

Read the value of the key

Sometimes one dot stands for more than one. A key tells you the value of a single dot. To find the total for a category: count the dots × the value of the key.

Team members · 1 dot = 5 people

Count the dots, then use the key

Baseball has the most dots. Choose a category and a key value to see how the total changes.

Same 7 dots, very different totals — that is why we always check the key first.

TeacherCommon trap. Students read the dots as the answer and ignore the key. Model the multiplication out loud: “7 dots, one dot is 5 people, so 7 × 5 = 35 people.”
Big idea 3 · Success criterion

Highest & lowest frequency

The highest frequency is the tallest stack of dots — the most common result. The lowest frequency is the shortest stack — the least common result. A number with no dots has a frequency of 0.

📈 Highest frequency

The category with the most dots — the tallest stack.

In the pets table: Cat, with 9.

📉 Lowest frequency

The category with the fewest dots — the shortest stack.

Reptile, with just 1 (Fish has 0).

🟰 Equal frequency

Two stacks the same height — equally common.

Compare stacks dot-for-dot.

➖ Difference

Subtract one frequency from another to compare.

Cat − Bird = 9 − 5 = 4 more.

TeacherKeep the language precise: “most / least common” describes the category; “highest / lowest frequency” describes the count. With a key, the tallest stack is still the highest frequency — remind students to multiply for the actual number.
Big idea 4 · Success criterion

Explain the data on a dot plot

A dot plot can come from a frequency table or from a list of results. Reading it means describing what the dots tell us — the most and least common results, gaps, and totals.

How many pets?
Pet typeHow many
🐶Dog7
🐱Cat9
🐦Bird5
🐠Fish0
🦎Reptile1

Each number in the “How many” column becomes a stack that many dots tall. Fish has 0 — an empty space.

Reading the pets data

  • Most common: Cat (highest frequency, 9).
  • Least common: Reptile (1) — Fish has none at all.
  • Difference: Cat has 9 − 7 = 2 more than Dog.
  • Total pets: 7 + 9 + 5 + 0 + 1 = 22.

Hours spent on homework a week — Year 5 students

The dot plot below shows how many hours each student spends on homework each week. 1 dot = 1 student. Use it to answer the questions.

Answer the questions

  1. How many students were surveyed?
  2. Which number of hours is most common?
  3. Which number of hours is least common?
  4. Are there any number of hours spent on homework a week the same? Which ones?
✓ Answers
  • Students surveyed: add every dot → 1 + 4 + 1 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 4 = 17 students.
  • Most common: 6 hours and 12 hours — both have the tallest stacks (4 students each).
  • Least common: 5, 7, 10 and 11 hours — each has only 1 student.
  • Same frequency: yes — 6 and 12 hours are the same (4 each), and 5, 7, 10 and 11 hours are the same (1 each).
TeacherPush justification: “How do you know?” → point to the height of the stack. Note there are two modes (6 and 12) — a good chance to say a data set can have more than one most-common value.
🟠 Amber Reading the data in dot plots 1 dot = 4 students

Ages of students at school

This dot plot shows the ages of students at a school. Watch the key: 1 dot = 4 students. Count the dots, then use the key.

Answer the questions

  1. How many students were surveyed?
  2. Which age is most common?
  3. Which age is least common?
  4. How many students are 9?
✓ Answers
  • Students surveyed: count all the dots (1 + 5 + 6 + 6 + 7 + 6 + 4 + 5 = 40 dots), then × 4 → 160 students.
  • Most common: age 9 — the tallest stack (7 dots).
  • Least common: age 5 — the shortest stack (1 dot).
  • How many are 9: 7 dots × 4 = 28 students.
TeacherThe key (1 dot = 4) is the whole point here. “Most / least common age” is answered by stack height, but every how many answer must be multiplied by 4. Model “7 dots × 4 = 28” before students start.
🔴 Red Interpreting dot plots Mrs Smith’s travel time

Minutes taken to get to school each day for 2 months — Mrs Smith

1 dot = 1 day. Use the dot plot to answer, then explain your thinking.

  1. How many days was data collected for?
  2. 14, 15 and 16 minutes occur the same number of times, as did 19, 20 and 21. Why do you think this could be?
  3. Could this person have changed their mode of transport over the month? Explain.
  4. Give a reason as to why you think 24 minutes might have occurred once.

✓ Answers & guidance
  • Days collected: add every dot → 1 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 1 = 30 days.
  • Same frequencies: the trips cluster into two groups (14–16 min and 19–21 min). This could happen if the travel time is fairly steady — the same route, mode or traffic gives similar times each day.
  • Changed transport? Possibly yes — the two separate clusters (a faster group around 14–16 and a slower group around 19–21) could mean a quicker mode on some days and a slower one on others. There is no single spread-out pattern, which is a clue.
  • 24 minutes once: a one-off outlier — e.g. heavy traffic, roadworks, bad weather, or leaving late that day.
  • Extension (1 dot = 2 days): every dot now counts double, so totals double (30 dots → 60 “days”). Accept any 3 sensible questions, e.g. “How many days took 20 minutes?” (5 dots × 2 = 10) or “What is the difference between the most and least common times?”.
TeacherQuestions 2–4 are about reasoning, not one right number — look for language of clusters, gaps and outliers (from the videos). For the extension, insist each student question includes the key in its answer, so they prove they know 1 dot = 2 days.
